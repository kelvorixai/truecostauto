5-year total cost of ownership: how to actually calculate it yourself

A sticker price tells you almost nothing about what a car actually costs to own. Total cost of ownership adds up five inputs over a fixed period — here's the method, generic enough to run on any car with your own numbers.

Total cost of ownership (TCO) is a standard method industry analysts use to compare vehicles on more than purchase price: it adds up every major cost of owning and running a car over a fixed period — commonly five years — into one number. Industry TCO studies covering a broad range of new vehicles have put the average annual cost of owning and running a new car in the low five figures over a five-year, moderate-mileage ownership period, with depreciation consistently the single largest line item. That average is useful context, but it's built from a blend of many different vehicles — the real value of the method is running it yourself, on your own numbers.

The five inputs

Every TCO calculation, regardless of vehicle type, is built from the same five components:

  • Depreciation — the loss in resale value between purchase and the end of your ownership period. Typically the single largest cost line by a wide margin.
  • Financing cost — total interest paid over a loan, or the opportunity cost of tying up cash if paying outright. Zero if you pay cash and count no opportunity cost, but real for almost everyone financing a purchase.
  • Insurance — your annual premium, multiplied out over the ownership period, adjusted for any expected changes (e.g., aging out of a higher-risk age band).
  • Maintenance and repairs — scheduled service plus a realistic allowance for unscheduled repairs, which rises as the vehicle ages and the factory warranty lapses.
  • Fuel or energy — annual mileage multiplied by cost per mile, whether that's gasoline, diesel, or electricity.

Step 1: Estimate depreciation

Depreciation is the resale or trade-in value you expect to receive at the end of the period, subtracted from the purchase price. New vehicles typically lose a substantial share of their value in the first year, with the rate of loss slowing in subsequent years — the exact percentages vary widely by vehicle, so the most reliable approach is to look up current used-market pricing for a comparable vehicle at your intended ownership age, rather than relying on a single industry-wide rule of thumb.

Step 2: Estimate financing cost

If financing, this is the total interest paid over your loan term — your lender or an online amortization calculator will give you this directly from your loan amount, rate, and term. If paying cash, some analysts still include an opportunity-cost line (what that cash could have earned elsewhere); whether to include it is a judgment call, but at minimum it's worth noting as a line you chose to exclude, for an apples-to-apples comparison against a financed alternative.

Step 3: Project insurance

Take your actual quote (or a realistic estimate for your rating profile) and multiply by the number of years in your ownership period. If you expect to move into a lower-risk age band or your record will meaningfully change (e.g., a young driver aging out of the highest-premium years), adjust later years down accordingly rather than holding the first-year premium flat across the whole period.

Step 4: Build a maintenance and repair allowance

Add up the manufacturer's scheduled maintenance costs for your ownership period — most manufacturers publish this — then add a realistic allowance for unscheduled repairs, which should increase in later years, especially once the factory warranty ends. A common approach is a low allowance in years one and two, rising in years three through five.

Step 5: Calculate fuel or energy cost

Multiply your expected annual mileage by your vehicle's cost per mile — miles per gallon (or per kWh) combined with local fuel or electricity prices. Use your actual expected annual mileage rather than a generic average if you know it, since this line scales linearly with how much you drive.

Step 6: Add it up and divide

Sum all five inputs over your ownership period for a total dollar figure, then divide by your total expected mileage over that period to get a cost-per-mile number — this is what makes it possible to compare two very different vehicles, or a five-year period against a seven-year one, on equal footing.
