EV vs. gas: true cost per mile, compared

"EVs are cheaper to run" is usually true, but the energy line is only one part of the comparison. Here's how energy, maintenance, insurance, and depreciation each contribute to the real cost-per-mile gap.

Cost per mile is a more useful comparison than sticker price alone, because it folds ownership costs into a single number you can scale to your own annual mileage. Building that number honestly means adding up four separate lines rather than stopping at the one — energy cost — that gets the most attention.

Energy cost: the line everyone compares

Based on typical home electricity rates and efficiency, EVs charged at home generally run around 5–8 cents per mile in electricity cost. Gas vehicles, at typical fuel prices and fuel-economy figures, generally run around 8–15 cents per mile, though this swings a lot with local gas prices and a given vehicle's MPG. On a typical ~12,000 annual miles, that's a rough range of roughly $700 a year in electricity versus $1,000–$1,800 a year in gasoline for a comparable vehicle — a meaningful gap, but not the whole picture. Public fast-charging, when used regularly instead of home charging, narrows this gap substantially and can sometimes erase it, since fast-charging rates run well above home electricity rates.

Maintenance: a real, structural EV advantage

This is where the EV advantage is most consistent. EVs have no oil changes, no spark plugs, no timing belts, and far fewer moving parts in the drivetrain overall — there's simply less to service. Regenerative braking also reduces brake pad and rotor wear substantially, since the electric motor handles much of the deceleration instead of friction brakes. Published estimates commonly put EV maintenance costs somewhere in the range of 30–40% lower than a comparable gas vehicle over time. Tires are the one category that can run higher for EVs, since the extra weight of the battery pack can increase tire wear.

Insurance: usually higher for EVs, and here's the mechanical reason why

EV insurance premiums typically run higher than a comparable gas vehicle — commonly cited estimates put the gap at roughly 10–20% higher, though some analyses have found considerably larger gaps depending on the specific models compared. The underlying reason is repair cost, not driving risk: EV battery packs and specialized components are expensive to replace, and even relatively minor collision damage can total out a battery pack, which pushes up the average claim severity insurers are pricing against.

Depreciation: historically the most volatile line

Depreciation is the hardest of the four lines to generalize, and it's the one that's moved the most in recent years. Early-generation EVs depreciated unusually fast because rapid improvements in battery range and charging technology made older models look outdated quickly, and uncertainty about battery longevity weighed on resale values. That dynamic has been shifting as the technology matures and the used-EV market grows, but it's still the least stable of the four inputs — worth treating as the most model- and year-specific of the four, rather than assuming today's pattern holds for every EV going forward.

A rough annual example

Stacking illustrative figures for a household driving about 12,000 miles a year makes the trade-off concrete. An EV might run roughly $700 in electricity, a comparable gas vehicle roughly $1,200–$1,500 in fuel — an EV energy advantage of several hundred dollars a year. Maintenance might run a few hundred dollars lower for the EV over the same period. Insurance might run $150–$300 a year higher for the EV. Depreciation is the wild card that can swing the total either direction depending on the specific vehicles being compared, which is exactly why it needs to be estimated from real resale data for the vehicles in question rather than assumed from a general rule. Add the four lines together with your own numbers, not these illustrative ones, before drawing a conclusion for your situation.

The energy and maintenance advantages are the most structurally reliable — they follow directly from how the two drivetrains work. The insurance and depreciation lines are more sensitive to the specific vehicle, model year, and local market, which is why a true cost-per-mile comparison should always be built from your own vehicle's numbers, not a single industry-wide average.
