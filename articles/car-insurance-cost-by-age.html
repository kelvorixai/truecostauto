The Numbers

Average car insurance cost by age (and why the curve looks the way it does)

Premiums drop fast through your twenties, flatten out in the middle of your working life, and creep back up later on. The shape isn't arbitrary — it's a direct readout of how insurers price risk.

Car insurance is priced almost entirely on expected claim cost: how likely a given driver is to file a claim, and how expensive that claim is likely to be, averaged across everyone who shares their rating profile. Age is one of the strongest single predictors an insurer has, which is why the age-to-premium curve is so consistent across carriers and states even though the exact dollar figures differ.

What the curve looks like

Based on published national averages for full coverage, the general shape looks roughly like this:

These are illustrative national averages for full coverage, not a quote — actual premiums vary heavily by state, driving record, vehicle, and individual insurer. The pattern is the point: a steep drop through the teens and early twenties, a long flat middle, and a moderate rise later in life.

Why teenage premiums are so high

Two things stack on top of each other for the youngest drivers. First, inexperience: crash rates per mile driven are highest in the first couple of years behind the wheel, largely because hazard recognition and vehicle control are skills that take real seat time to build, not something a license test fully certifies. Second, teen drivers as a group have a higher rate of higher-severity crashes — more speeding, more night driving, more distracted driving relative to more experienced drivers — which pushes up the average claim payout, not just the claim frequency. Insurers price both the "how often" and the "how much," and for teens both numbers run high at the same time.

Why the curve falls so fast through the twenties

Experience compounds quickly in the first few years of driving — most of the risk reduction from "having driven before" happens early, which is why the drop from age 18 to age 25 is so much larger than the drop from 25 to 35. Age itself is also doing double duty as a rough proxy for a set of correlated factors insurers can't observe directly: driving in more predictable patterns, fewer high-risk trip types, and a claims history (or lack of one) starting to build up. By the mid-to-late twenties, most of that early-risk premium has already been priced out.

Why the middle years are the cheapest

Drivers roughly in their 40s and 50s post the lowest claim frequency and, on average, the lowest claim severity of any age band. This is less about age itself and more about what tends to correlate with it at a population level: an established driving history, generally lower rates of the riskiest driving behaviors, and vehicles that are, on average, driven somewhat differently than in the higher-mileage, higher-risk-trip years of early adulthood. This is the floor of the curve — the age band every other band is being compared against.

Why premiums tick back up later in life

The increase for older drivers isn't about experience — it reflects a different set of risk factors that become statistically more common with age: slower reaction times, vision and hearing changes, and a higher rate of injury (and therefore higher claim cost) when a crash of the same severity does occur. Insurers aren't pricing in a judgment about any individual driver's ability — they're pricing the average outcome for the age band, the same way they do for every other group.

Why this matters even though it's not "advice"

Understanding the mechanics doesn't tell you which insurer to choose — that depends on your specific driving record, vehicle, location, and the quotes you actually receive. What it does explain is why a premium changes the way it does as you or a household member moves through these age bands, and why comparing your quote to a "national average" only tells you part of the story — the other part is which risk factors, beyond age, are being priced into your specific quote.
